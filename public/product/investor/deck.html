THE SOLUTION

The bet is sequencing: prove retention on a free, ad-funded loop — the business is already legal and already the default.

  • Retention is still the gate. Monetisation is no longer downstream of a licence: rewarded ads on a coin economy need none, in the US, EU or India (§8.1).
  • The loop: watch a short ad → coins; enter a duel; the house pays the winner a prize. No coin ever moves between players. No purchase. No cash-out.
  • Every competitor that scaled is drowning in one complaint cluster — ads after every question, unskippable, lives walls, hidden bots (§8.6). Ours are opt-in only, priced before the tap, never inside a round.
  • We have measured no session length and no retention benefit (numbers.md §5.15, §7.1). The economy is built and tested; the number it depends on is not.
Speaker notes
The claim on this slide that matters is "needs no licence", so be able to say where it comes from: the legal research of 14 September (docs/money/legal-merge.json, six lanes, audited) found that a closed-loop coin economy with no purchase and no prize of value is outside every real-money rule read in the US, the EU and India, and the ad-tech lane of 16 September found the same for Google's publisher gambling policy. The residual policy risk is different and smaller — Google's rewarded-ad policy requires rewards to be non-transferable, which is why the copy says "the house pays a prize" and never "take your opponent's coins" — and it is on the risks slide. On the competitor complaint cluster, the review evidence is verbatim and sourced in docs/money/ads/lane-competitors.json: Trivia Crack ("Every missed question, every screen change, pretty much anything you do results in an ad"), Trivia Star, Jeopardy!, Sporcle and every mass-market football quiz app. The two products that died — QuizUp and HQ Trivia — died of no monetisation, in the founders' own words. That is the whole argument for monetising from day one with the one format users say they tolerate: rewarded video, which mobile gamers prefer to interstitials roughly four to one (Mistplay, vendor figure, likely). The economy is built: a pure reducer with the researched configuration, tested at fifteen scenarios plus a property test, with a config sanitiser that structurally cannot carry a difficulty or bot-skill field — because a loss-streak "mercy" that softens questions is the conduct the FTC's dark-pattern orders describe. Say that if integrity comes up.

RISKS

The risks that emptied this category still lead — and the ad model adds four of its own.

  • Long-tail retention. Global median D30 across 16,000+ mobile games is 0.68-0.79% (GameAnalytics est., CY2025). Trivia Royale reported 45% D1 and closed in eight months.
  • Opponent integrity. Skillz v. Papaya: a $420M jury verdict (Apr 2026) and $719M disgorgement (Jul 2026) — the court found it "undisputed" Papaya ran bots while denying it 200+ times.
  • Ad policy. Google requires rewarded-ad rewards to be non-transferable. Our entry-and-prize flow is designed to satisfy it; a written answer from Google is the open question we must get first.
  • No web verification. No web ad network verifies a completion server-side (Google, verbatim). Web rewards are cheap and capped by design; verified rewards need the store build (W8).
  • Web eCPM. Every published rewarded eCPM is in-app; web is quoted 30-70% lower, and ad-blocking runs ~30% of users. India eCPM is unverified anywhere reachable.
  • Paid acquisition never pays back. Ad LTV per install is $0.06-0.25 in the US and about a cent in India against CPIs of $2.50-5.00. Growth must be organic and calendar-driven.
  • Benchmark transfer and content depth. All retention benchmarks are app-store installs, not web (§5.14). 329 shipped (60-66 per sport); 300 per sport is the launch gate.
  • Approval risk. AdSense will not approve a github.io subdomain; H5 Games Ads is application-only and "not guaranteed". A first-party domain is the first item on the critical path.
Speaker notes
Eight risks, and the order is deliberate: the two that emptied the category first, then the four the ad model introduces, then the two carried over. Papaya replaces AviaGames as the integrity example because it is larger and more recent: the SDNY jury found for Skillz under the Lanham Act on 23 April 2026 ($420M) and the 28 July 2026 judgment added $719M disgorgement as an alternative remedy the plaintiff may elect — it is a district-court judgment subject to appeal, binding nobody, and still the most decision-relevant fact about bots in this genre. Our answer is structural: every opponent card is badged Human / Practice bot / Ghost, bots never play for coins, and the trust page publishes the bot policy. The ad-policy risk is the one to rehearse. Google's Policies for ad units that offer rewards say a reward must be non-transferable — "only redeemable and usable by the same user who received it, and is not ... items that can be transferred to a third party" (confirmed verbatim by the audit). A coin earned from an ad, staked into a pot the opponent wins, has arguably been transferred. Our flow is entry-to-the-house and prize-from-the-house, sized equal, so no coin moves between players and the copy never says "take their coins". We believe that satisfies the clause; we have not been told so by Google, and the launch plan sends that enquiry before the soft launch. An account-level disable would end the model overnight, which is why it is on this slide and not in a footnote. "No web verification" is a quote, not a paraphrase: "Server-side verification is an app only feature and it is unavailable for web use." Consequence: a web reward is treated as spoofable and kept cheap, with a server-issued nonce, a completion window no shorter than the ad, and daily caps — the ceiling on a lying client is one payout per nonce the server chose to issue. Verified rewards arrive with the Capacitor/TWA builds and AdMob's signed callbacks in week 8. The paid-acquisition line is the launch lane's headline and it is arithmetic, not opinion: ad LTV per install of roughly $0.06 (median retention) to $0.25 (90 days, good curve) in the US, about a cent in India, against 2026 CPIs of $2.50-5.00 North America and $1.50-3.00 APAC. APPENDIX — THE MEASUREMENT LAYER

How retention is measured, what is not, and why the rate still needs players.

  • Per device: install day, active days, session count, engaged time and four counters — duel rounds, matches, expedition cards, quests (lib/analytics.mjs, 24 tests).
  • Per device: D1 / D7 / D30 as true, false, or null while the window is still open. A test asserts an unfinished window can never read as a miss.
  • One device is a sample of one; no export is shaped like a rate, and the screen says so. No third-party SDK, nothing personal, no network call; the player exports JSON or CSV, or erases, at /analytics.
  • The cohort rate comes from an opt-in anonymous daily ping (cohort-ping / cohort-report, table cohort_days, 20 tests): aggregate-only, no per-device row, buckets under five devices suppressed.
  • What none of this gives us today: a retention rate. That needs players. The instrument is what shipped; the cohort is what the round buys.
Speaker notes
This page exists so that the claim on slide 8 can be interrogated rather than believed. It is numbers.md §7.1 in slide form and it should be read as a design, not as a result. The device-local half is a pure module with no storage, no network call and no third-party SDK, which is why the privacy properties are checkable by reading one file rather than by trusting a vendor: it records install day, the days the device was active, session count under a stated inactivity threshold that the app names on screen, engaged time counted only while the app is on screen, and four in-app counters, then answers whether this device came back on day 1, 7 or 30. Both of those conventions are chosen rather than standard and the app names them on screen; say so before someone asks how a session is defined. The null return is the part worth dwelling on with a numerate investor — a window that has not finished reads as null rather than as a miss, which is the single easiest way to flatter or wreck an early retention number, and a test asserts it. The cohort half is the only part that can ever produce a rate, it is opt-in, the identifier is anonymous, the report can return no per-device row by construction, and any bucket describing fewer than five devices is suppressed rather than rounded — which is also a real limit on how early the first number can be published, and it is deliberate. The corresponding weakness is on slide 11 as a named risk: an opt-in denominator is consenting devices, not all devices, and the first published cohort figure has to carry its consent rate next to it. Twenty-four tests cover the device module and twenty the cohort endpoints; quote those two counts only, because they are the two that §7.1 records. Finally, the honest close: none of this is traction and none of it is a number. §7.3 is explicit that there are still no users, no revenue, no cohort, no CAC, no LTV and no ARPDAU, and that every figure in §4 remains a plan.
