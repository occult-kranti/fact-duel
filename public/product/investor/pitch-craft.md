# How to structure and write a seed pitch deck
*Synthesised from YC Startup Library, Sequoia, and DocSend/Dropbox telemetry. Every figure below carries the URL it came from; where a claim is my own arithmetic or synthesis rather than a sourced claim, it is marked **[derived]** or **[unsourced]**.*

---

## 1. First: you are writing two decks, not one

Almost every contradiction between the sources dissolves once you separate these.

| | **Sent deck** (emailed, read alone, no narrator) | **Spoken deck** (meeting / Demo Day) |
|---|---|---|
| Length | **19–20 pages** ([DocSend](https://web.archive.org/web/20240302210508/https://www.docsend.com/blog/what-vcs-really-want-to-see-inside-your-seed-deck/)); successful seed raises averaged **20 slides**, "unchanged" year over year ([DocSend 2020](https://web.archive.org/web/20200930105556/https://www.docsend.com/blog/a-brief-anatomy-of-a-successful-seed-raise/)) | **5–7 slides**: "Since you only have 5-7 ideas you want to get across to investors, you shouldn't have too many slides." ([Kevin Hale, YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)) |
| Time budget | **2:18–3:44** of silent skimming (see §3) | **"You only have 2 minutes and 30 seconds."** ([Hale](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)) |
| Job | Survive being read with no one defending it | Keep attention on **you**, not the screen |

The YC seed template reconciles the two: it is **1 fixed title slide + 9 expandable sets**. "Any of the other slides in this deck should be treated as a first slide of a set. If you can keep the set to n=1, that's ideal, but if you need more, that's ok. You probably don't want any set here where n > 3." ([Aaron Harris, YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2u-how-to-build-your-seed-round-pitch-deck)). **[derived]** 1 + (9 sets × ~2 slides) ≈ 19 — which lands exactly on DocSend's 19–20. Build the 10-slot spine, expand only where you must, cap any set at 3.

**Slide count answer: 10 sections / ~19–20 physical pages for the sent deck; 5–7 for the spoken one.** Note the 3-slide cap is Harris's recommendation, not a hard rule.

## 2. The exact slide order, with a one-line brief each

Baseline order is DocSend's 12 sections, because it is the only order with an outcome claim attached to it: "founders who open their decks with the first four sections above are more likely to raise funding than those who opt for other approaches" ([DocSend](https://web.archive.org/web/20240302210508/https://www.docsend.com/blog/what-vcs-really-want-to-see-inside-your-seed-deck/)). One deliberate deviation is flagged at slide 7.

| # | Slide | One-line brief | VC dwell |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Title / company purpose** | Company name, logo, and one declarative sentence — Sequoia: *"define your company in a single declarative sentence. This is harder than it looks."* Include contact info. This is the one slot Harris says must stay at n=1. | 26s |
| 2 | **Problem** | Whose pain, how acute, what they do today instead — concrete and specific, not a macro trend. Non-negotiable: **92%** of successful pre-seed decks had one (**100%** of US West Coast ones). | 34s |
| 3 | **Solution** | What you built and why it removes that exact pain; one sentence plus one image beats a paragraph. | 34s |
| 4 | **Market size** | The number, the method, and the evidence — in that order (see §4). | 29s |
| 5 | **Why now** | What changed in the world (tech, regulation, cost curve, behaviour) that makes this possible/inevitable *this year* — Sequoia: *"Why now?"* | 23s |
| 6 | **Product** | Show the thing working: screenshots, a flow, the core loop. Second-longest read; earn it. | 59s |
| 7 | **Traction** | The strongest real evidence someone wants this — put it here, before competition. **[deviation]** DocSend orders competition 7th and traction 8th; YC's template puts traction **4th**, straight after solution, and DocSend separately finds hidden traction gets punished (§5). Do not bury it. | 40s |
| 8 | **Business model** | How a dollar arrives and what it costs to get it. **Longest look of any slide** — DocSend: *"This section gets the longest look from VCs."* | 64s |
| 9 | **Competition / landscape** | Direct and indirect alternatives, and the one axis on which you win — Sequoia asks for competitors *plus* the winning strategy. | 34s |
| 10 | **Team** | Why *this* team, on *this* problem: the specific unfair insight or earned experience. YC's insight slot ("what makes you special / your insights") folds naturally here or into slide 5. | 38s |
| 11 | **Financials** | At seed, minimal — see §5. | 37s |
| 12 | **The ask** | How much, for how many months of runway, and what it buys. | 32s |

**Where the three canonical lists differ** (all defensible; don't agonise):
- **YC seed template, 10 slides**: title → problem → solution → traction → more metrics → what makes you special/insights → business model → market → team → ask ([Harris](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2u-how-to-build-your-seed-round-pitch-deck)).
- **YC seed fundraising guide, 12 parts**: company/logo/tagline → vision → problem → customer → solution → market → market landscape → traction → business model → team → summary → fundraising — explicitly prefaced *"There is no fixed format or order, but the following parts are usually present."* ([Ralston](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)).
- **Sequoia**: company purpose → problem → solution → why now → market potential → competition → business model → team → financials → vision ([Sequoia](https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan/)). It adds the two slots YC's template omits: **why now** and a closing **vision** ("what you'll have built in five years").

Add a closing vision/summary slide if your story needs a landing; it costs one page.

## 3. The evidence on investor attention

**Total time on deck — the trend is down, and the latest number is the one to design against:**

| Year | Avg. review time | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 2015 | 3:44 | [DocSend Startup Index](https://web.archive.org/web/20200930105556/https://www.docsend.com/blog/a-brief-anatomy-of-a-successful-seed-raise/) |
| 2019 | 3:27 ("steadily declined throughout 2020") | same |
| 2019 pre-seed | 3:21 successful / 3:30 unsuccessful | [DocSend pre-seed](https://web.archive.org/web/20251007221137/https://www.docsend.com/blog/pre-seed-pitch-deck-guide/) |
| undated | 3:20 | [DocSend seed deck guide](https://web.archive.org/web/20240302210508/https://www.docsend.com/blog/what-vcs-really-want-to-see-inside-your-seed-deck/) |
| **2024 (latest published)** | **2:18** — VCs "raced through pitch decks at an average of 2 minutes, 18 seconds" (10 Jun 2024) | [DocSend Pitch Deck Interest](https://web.archive.org/web/20260525121043/https://www.docsend.com/pitch-deck-metrics/) |

DocSend stopped publishing the Time Spent metric after 10 June 2024 and stopped updating the index entirely after 23 September 2024, so **2:18 is the most recent published figure that exists** — there is no 2025 or 2026 reading. **[derived]** 2:18 across a 20-slide deck is **~7 seconds per slide**. Design every page to be comprehensible in one glance at that speed.

**Per-section averages** (Business model 64s · Product 59s · Traction 40s · Team 38s · Financials 37s · Problem 34s · Solution 34s · Competition 34s · Ask 32s · Market size 29s · Company purpose 26s · Why now 23s) — [source](https://web.archive.org/web/20260622084020/https://www.docsend.com/blog/what-vcs-really-want-to-see-inside-your-seed-deck/).

**Use these as a ranking, not a budget.** They sum to 7m30s against DocSend's own stated 3m44s average total — they are non-additive per-section averages, not a decomposition of one session. The reliable signal is the *ordering*: business model and product get read closely; why now, company purpose and market size get skimmed hardest.

**Section frequency** (2019 pre-seed, n=174): problem slide in 92% of successful decks; ~1.8 slides on problem and ~2.8 on product (the same article's data boxes say 1.9 and 2.7 — a precision mismatch worth noting); traction present in ~60% (59% per data box). [Source](https://web.archive.org/web/20251007221137/https://www.docsend.com/blog/pre-seed-pitch-deck-guide/).

**Evidence quality — state this to yourself before you over-fit:** all DocSend figures are proprietary first-party product telemetry from opt-in users (n=174 pre-seed 2019; n=175 seed), not peer-reviewed or audited; the 3:20 figure carries no published sample size or date range; the per-section table has internal inconsistencies. YC's numbers are undated editorial rules of thumb. Treat all of it as directional.

**Process context**, since the deck is only half the job: successful 2019 seed founders contacted **77** investors and held **40** meetings; unfunded founders contacted 70 and held **15**. The honest reading is that *meetings held stayed flat* — 40 in both 2015 and 2019 — while outreach rose from 58 to 77; the discriminator is conversion, not volume ([source](https://web.archive.org/web/20200930105556/https://www.docsend.com/blog/a-brief-anatomy-of-a-successful-seed-raise/)).

## 4. TAM / SAM / SOM credibility rules

The market slide gets **29 seconds** — third-least-read in the deck. It has to survive a skim and a later sanity check by someone who knows the sector.

**Sourced rules:**

1. **Aim for TAM > $1B, and prove it.** YC: *"The (huge) Market you are addressing — Total Available Market (TAM) >$1B if possible. Include the most persuasive evidence you have that this is real."* The second sentence is the whole rule; the number without the evidence is worthless. ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising))
2. **An unjustified big number is an explicit DON'T.** YC's "What Not to Do While Communicating with Investors" appendix lists 17 DON'Ts including *"Use ridiculous / silly market size numbers without clear justification"* — alongside *"Be dishonest in any way."* A hand-wavy $50B TAM does not read as ambition; it reads as a credibility failure on the one slide where you were asked to show your work. ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising))
3. **Name the customer before the number.** Sequoia: *"Identify your customer and your market. Some of the best companies invent their own markets."* A market defined as "identify the customer × what they'll pay" is checkable; "the global X industry" is not.
4. **Give it a landscape slot.** YC's 12-part list separates "The (huge) Market" from "Market Landscape" — size and structure are two different questions. ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising))

**Practical construction — [unsourced]:** I could not fetch a primary source prescribing a bottom-up-vs-top-down method (my web search budget was exhausted before I could verify one), so treat the following as craft convention, not cited evidence:
- Build **bottom-up** — (number of identifiable buyers) × (realistic annual price) — and show the arithmetic on the slide in one line. A top-down analyst number can appear as corroboration, never as the primary claim.
- If you cite an analyst or market-research figure, **name the firm and the year and label it an estimate**. Unattributed market numbers are the single easiest thing for an investor to discount.
- **SAM** = the slice your product and go-to-market can actually reach today. **SOM** = what you can plausibly win in the funding period — this is the number that should reconcile with your own traction and ask.
- If TAM, SAM and SOM don't reconcile with slide 7 and slide 12, an investor who does the arithmetic will find the gap.

## 5. Pre-revenue: traction and financials, honestly

**Traction — the failure mode is hiding it, not lacking it.**

DocSend: *"We found that VCs spend 80 percent more time evaluating the traction of companies that didn't raise money successfully. If your market traction isn't immediate to investors, it may raise skepticism and prompt more inspection that may not work out in your favor."* ([source](https://web.archive.org/web/20240302210508/https://www.docsend.com/blog/what-vcs-really-want-to-see-inside-your-seed-deck/))

The lesson is counterintuitive and important: **extra scrutiny is the symptom of a vague traction slide, not the cause of rejection.** Ambiguity invites digging. A thin but unambiguous slide outperforms an impressive-looking one that requires re-reading.

What legitimately counts pre-revenue, per DocSend: *"Investors will take into account the stage your product is in. You can include letters of intent, testimonials, customer pipeline, and beta feedback as appropriate to your product's stage."* DocSend's current wording is *"letters of intent, design partner commitments, beta user feedback, waitlist signups, or early revenue."*

Rules for writing it:
- Label the stage on the slide. "Pre-revenue, 6 design partners signed" is credible; a chart with an unlabelled y-axis is not.
- Show **rate of change**, not just cumulative totals. Cumulative-only charts read as concealment.
- Never present a waitlist as users, an LOI as a contract, or a pilot as revenue. YC's DON'T list opens with *"Be dishonest in any way"* — and a seed investor who catches one inflated label discounts the entire deck.
- Roughly 40% of pre-seed decks had **no** traction section at all (~60% included one). Not having one is survivable; faking one is not.

**Financials — keep them minimal, on purpose.**

YC explicitly lists *"Create detailed financials"* among its 17 DON'Ts, and adds: *"Do not spend too much time developing diligence documents for a seed round. If an investor is asking for too much due diligence or financials, they are almost certainly someone to avoid."* ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising))

So: a financials slide, yes (DocSend sees VCs spend ~37s there) — but scoped to what YC specifies for the executive summary: **"minimum financials (revenue, if any, and fundraising prior and current)."** Revenue to date (say zero if zero), money raised so far, current burn, months of runway. Not a five-year hockey-stick P&L. If you show projections at all, show the assumptions that drive them and keep it to one slide.

**The ask — YC's arithmetic:**
- *"Simply answer that you are raising for N months (usually 12-18) and will thus need $X, where X will usually be between $500k and $1.5 million."*
- Runway maths: *"an engineer… costs all-in about $15k per month. So, if you would like to be funded for 18 months of operations with an average of five engineers, then you will need about 15k x 5 x 18 = $1.35mm."*
- Dilution: *"If you can manage to give up as little as 10% of your company in your seed round, that is wonderful, but most rounds will require up to 20% dilution and you should try to avoid more than 25%."*

⚠️ **These are YC's undated editorial rules of thumb, not measured or inflation-indexed figures.** The $15k/engineer/month and the $500k–$1.5M band are very likely understated as of September 2026; I found no current primary source to reprice them. Use the *structure* (N months × team × cost = ask; dilution 10/20/25) and substitute your own current, defensible cost base. Do not quote YC's dollar figures as if they were 2026 market data.

**Separate document:** the executive summary is not the deck. YC: *"The executive summary should be one or two pages (one is better) and should include vision, product, team (location, contact info), traction, market size, and minimum financials."* The **slide deck** is what YC calls the coherent leave-behind.

## 6. Top failure modes

**Design failures** — Hale's three rules are *"Make it legible. Make it simple. Make it obvious."* and his framing is the thing to internalise: *"Investors invest in teams not slides. Your slides should make your ideas more clear. Don't let your slides distract investors from what you're saying out loud. You want them to be impressed by you. Not your slides."* His nine named distractions ([source](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)):

> Too much text · Excessive explanations and caveats · Excessive branding per slide · Photos with no titles or captions · Animations · Transitions · Memes · Subtle humor · Accidental humor

**Content and process failures**, ranked by how often they kill a seed deck:

1. **Vague or buried traction** — triggers 80% more scrutiny, which is the thing that goes wrong, not the thin numbers themselves.
2. **An unjustified market number** — YC DON'T: *"Use ridiculous / silly market size numbers without clear justification."* Fastest way to lose a reader who knows the sector.
3. **Any dishonesty or inflated label** — YC DON'T: *"Be dishonest in any way."* Unrecoverable once caught.
4. **Over-building financials** — YC DON'T: *"Create detailed financials."* At seed it signals misallocated effort, not rigour.
5. **Explaining the obvious** — YC DON'T: *"Spend time on the obvious."* At ~7 seconds a slide **[derived]**, every line spent on the self-evident costs you a line you needed.
6. **No problem slide, or a generic one** — 92% of successful pre-seed decks had one; 100% on the US West Coast.
7. **A weak business model slide** — it gets the *longest* look (64s) of any section. Under-investing here is the most common mismatch between author effort and reader attention.
8. **Too many slides / uncapped sets** — expand past 3 slides in any section and you are past the reader's budget.
9. **A deck that needs a narrator** — the sent deck is read alone, in silence, in about two minutes.
10. **Chasing an over-diligencing seed investor** — YC: *"If an investor is asking for too much due diligence or financials, they are almost certainly someone to avoid."*
11. **Confusing outreach with conversion** — meetings held stayed flat at 40 while outreach climbed from 58 to 77; more emails is not the fix for a deck that isn't converting.
